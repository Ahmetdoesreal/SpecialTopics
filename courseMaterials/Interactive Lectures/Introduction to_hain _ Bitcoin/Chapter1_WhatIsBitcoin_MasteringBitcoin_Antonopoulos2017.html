Introduction to Blockchain & Bitcoin

A comprehensive overview of the fundamental concepts, technologies, and practical applications of blockchain and its first major use case, Bitcoin.

What is Bitcoin?

Bitcoin is a collection of concepts and technologies that form the basis of a digital money ecosystem. Units of currency called bitcoin are used to store and transmit value among participants in the bitcoin network. Users can transfer bitcoin over the network to do just about anything that can be done with conventional currencies, including buy and sell goods, send money to people or organizations, or extend credit.

Unlike traditional currencies, bitcoin are entirely virtual. There are no physical coins. The coins are implied in transactions that transfer value from sender to recipient. Users of bitcoin own keys that allow them to prove ownership of bitcoin. With these keys they can sign transactions to unlock the value and spend it by transferring it to a new owner.

A Digital Money Ecosystem:

Bitcoin is a distributed, peer-to-peer system. As such there is no “central” server or point of control. Bitcoin are created through a process called “mining,” which involves competing to find solutions to a mathematical problem while processing bitcoin transactions. Essentially, bitcoin mining decentralizes the currency-issuance and clearing functions of a central bank.

Behind the scenes, bitcoin is also the name of the protocol, a peer-to-peer network, and a distributed computing innovation. The bitcoin currency is really only the first application of this invention. Bitcoin represents the culmination of decades of research in cryptography and distributed systems.

Lecture Content

Cryptography is essential for digital money. It helps answer three fundamental questions: Is the money authentic? Can it be spent only once? Who owns it? Blockchain relies on two key cryptographic concepts: Hash Functions and Public-Key Cryptography.

Hash Functions

A hash function is a one-way mathematical function that takes an input of any size and produces a fixed-size output (e.g., 256 bits for SHA-256). It has key properties like being deterministic (same input always gives same output) and the "avalanche effect," where a tiny change in input creates a completely different output.

import hashlib
# Example 1: Basic hashing
text1 = "Hello, Blockchain!"
hash1 = hashlib.sha256(text1.encode()).hexdigest()
print(f"Hash of '{text1}':")
print(hash1)

# Example 2: Avalanche effect
text2 = "Hello, Blockchain." # Note the period
hash2 = hashlib.sha256(text2.encode()).hexdigest()
print(f"\\nHash of '{text2}':")
print(hash2)

Public-Key Cryptography

This is an asymmetric system using a key pair: a private key and a public key. The private key is kept secret and is used to sign transactions, proving ownership. The public key is derived from the private key and is shared with others to verify signatures. A Bitcoin address is derived from the public key, acting like a bank account number.

The Byzantine Generals' Problem

This is a classic distributed computing problem: how can a group of generals surrounding a city agree on a plan of action if they can only communicate via messengers and some generals may be traitors? Bitcoin solves this with its Proof-of-Work (PoW) consensus mechanism. PoW requires participants (miners) to solve a difficult computational puzzle. The first to solve it gets to propose the next block of transactions and is rewarded. This makes it economically infeasible for traitors (malicious actors) to disrupt the network, as it costs more to attack than to participate honestly.

Traditional systems require trust in intermediaries like banks. Blockchain creates a "trustless" system where you don't trust institutions, you trust mathematics and verification. "Don't trust, verify" is the motto.

Bitcoin's Four Key Innovations

Bitcoin's success comes from combining four key technologies in a unique way:

  1. A decentralized peer-to-peer network: No central server, resilient to attack.
  2. A public transaction ledger (the blockchain): An immutable, transparent history of all transactions.
  3. A set of rules for independent transaction validation: Each node validates transactions independently based on the protocol rules.
  4. A mechanism for reaching consensus (Proof-of-Work): Ensures the integrity and chronological order of the ledger.

When Does Blockchain Make Sense?

Blockchain is not a silver bullet. It's best suited for situations needing transparency, where multiple parties don't fully trust each other, there's no natural central authority, and censorship resistance is important. Traditional databases are often better for high-throughput, private, or reversible transactions.

Use Case Analysis: Real World Stories

Charitable Donations: Eugenia's Story

Eugenia runs a children’s charity in the Philippines. To increase transparency for donors, she uses bitcoin. This allows every donation to be tracked on a public ledger, showing donors exactly how funds are allocated and addressing concerns about administrative costs.

Import/Export: Mohammed's Story

Mohammed, an electronics importer in Dubai, uses bitcoin for large B2B payments to suppliers in the US and China. This replaces slow (3-5 day) and expensive ($45) wire transfers with transactions that confirm in about an hour for a much lower fee, accelerating his entire import process.

Banking the Unbanked: Gabriel's Story

Gabriel, a teenager in Rio de Janeiro, runs a web store selling bitcoin-branded merchandise. Too young for a bank account, he uses bitcoin to receive payments from customers globally, empowering his entrepreneurial spirit without needing permission from a traditional financial institution.

Common Misconceptions

  • "Blockchain = Bitcoin": False. Bitcoin is an *application* of blockchain technology. Blockchain is the underlying distributed ledger technology that has many other uses.
  • "Blockchain is anonymous": False. It's pseudonymous. Transactions are public, and if an address is linked to an identity, all associated activity can be traced.
  • "Blockchain is unhackable": The chain itself is incredibly secure, but wallets, exchanges, and user practices can be vulnerable.

Post-Lecture Review

Summary

  • Blockchain solves the double-spend problem without a central authority using distributed consensus.
  • The trust model is fundamentally different: trust math and verification, not institutions.
  • There are trade-offs: you gain decentralization and transparency but lose scalability and reversibility.
  • All Bitcoin uses blockchain, but not all blockchain is Bitcoin.

Getting Started with Bitcoin

Getting started is easier than you think. The most common user interface is a "bitcoin wallet," similar to how a web browser is an interface for the internet. There are many types of wallets (desktop, mobile, web, hardware), but a mobile wallet is often best for beginners.

Alice's First Transaction

Let's follow Alice, a new user. She downloads a mobile wallet like Mycelium. The app automatically creates a new wallet for her, which includes a private key (kept secret) and a Bitcoin address (which she can share).

Her friend Joe wants to send her $10 worth of bitcoin.

  1. Alice opens her wallet and selects "Receive". This displays her Bitcoin address as a long string of characters and a scannable QR code.
  2. Joe opens his wallet and selects "Send". He scans Alice's QR code to get her address.
  3. He enters $10, and his wallet calculates the equivalent amount in bitcoin (e.g., 0.10 BTC).
  4. He confirms the transaction. His wallet signs it with his private key and broadcasts it to the network.
  5. Within seconds, Alice's wallet sees the incoming "Unconfirmed" transaction. After about 10 minutes, it gets included in a block and becomes "Confirmed".

Alice is now the owner of 0.10 BTC. The transaction is irreversible and recorded permanently on the blockchain. She can now spend it at merchants who accept bitcoin.
